# Round two: enterprise emails, marketing agencies, charities

Researched 2 August 2026.

## TLT and JLL — you're right, send them

My earlier answer conflated two different things and I should correct it.

**The cost of one email is two minutes.** At even a 1% hit rate on a £25K deal
that is £250 of expected value for two minutes of work, which is obviously
worth doing. There was never a good argument against sending the email itself.

What I was actually arguing against — and should have said plainly — is
**building a pipeline around them**. The cost of enterprise targets is never
the email. It is the fortnight afterwards: the proposal nobody asked for, the
follow-ups, the "they said they'd take it to the team", the mental space it
occupies while clinics that would have replied in two days go uncontacted.

So: **send them, then forget them.** If a reply comes, brilliant. If not, it
cost two minutes.

### Two things that change the odds materially

**Do not email a generic address.** A cold email to `info@` or a marketing
inbox at a 90,000-person company is not a low-probability play, it is a
zero-probability one — it lands with someone who has no authority and no
incentive to forward it. Find a named person: innovation, operations, IT or
digital lead, on LinkedIn. One named human beats ten generic inboxes.

**Use the building.** For JLL this is the whole game and almost nobody else
has it. A subject line reading *"Tenant in [your building] — quick question"*
is not a cold email, it is a local one. It will get opened. That single fact
moves JLL from your worst-odds target to a genuinely reasonable one, and it is
the only reason I would prioritise them over another clinic.

The same logic is weaker for TLT — being a Bristol firm is not a relationship —
but a named partner or their innovation lead is still far better than a
switchboard inbox.

**What I would not do:** write a bespoke proposal, chase more than once, or
count any of them in your forecast.

## Marketing agencies — poor clients, possible partners

Read the sources on this one carefully, because almost every article
recommending marketing agencies as an AI niche **is published by a company
selling white-label AI to marketing agencies**. That is marketing content, not
neutral analysis. The useful facts underneath it:

- Digital marketing agencies run **15–25% net margins**
- White-label AI platforms let an agency launch a branded AI service in
  **30–60 days** at **30–70% margins** — GoHighLevel, Vendasta and others exist
  precisely to make this trivial
  ([Enrich Labs](https://www.enrichlabs.ai/blog/marketing-automation-for-agencies-2026-playbook),
  [Am I Cited](https://www.amicited.com/blog/white-label-ai-visibility-tools-agencies/))

That combination is bad news for selling *to* them:

1. **They are your competitors.** An agency that wants AI receptionists for its
   clients white-labels one and resells it. They are on the supply side of this
   market, not the demand side.
2. **They know your costs.** Selling automation to people who sell automation
   means every price is scrutinised by someone who could build it.
3. **Margins are thin.** 15–25% net is closer to cleaning than to dentistry.

**The reframe worth considering:** agencies are a plausible *channel*, not a
customer. An agency with fifty local clients and no AI capability could resell
your build under their brand. That is a partnership conversation with different
economics — lower price per deal, far higher volume, and they do the selling.
Worth one exploratory conversation, not a campaign.

## Charities — real, but only the large ones

The £25K anecdote is entirely plausible. The sector data explains both why it
happened and why it is not repeatable at the small end.

**What supports it:**

- **79% of charities now use AI, rising to 92% among larger organisations** —
  so the concept needs no selling
- **81% report digital progress this year**, up from 60% in 2025
- Large charities run enormous administrative loads: donor management, grant
  applications, volunteer coordination, beneficiary intake — all repetitive,
  all automatable
- Cancer Research UK alone put **an extra £24M** into supporter-focused digital
  transformation
  ([Charity Digital Skills Report 2026](https://fundraising.co.uk/2026/07/13/charity-digital-skills-report-2026-ai-use-hits-79-as-funders-urged-to-act-fast/))

**What kills it below a certain size:**

- **63% say squeezed finances are their single biggest barrier** to digital
  transformation
- Only **17% received digital funding from grant makers this year, down from
  30%** — the funding is going the wrong way
- There is **very little dedicated UK AI funding for non-profits**

So the sector splits hard. A £500K-income charity has no budget and a trustee
board that will question spending donor money on software. A £10M+ charity has
a digital team, a transformation budget, and 92% likelihood of already using AI.

**Where the £25K deals live:** large charities, and specifically
**project-funded work**. Charities frequently hold restricted grant money
earmarked for a named project. If your automation is scoped as part of that
project rather than as general overhead, it comes out of a budget that already
exists and is not competing with frontline spend. That framing is the whole
trick, and it is why one person can quote £25K to a charity and get it.

**Caveats to price in:** trustee governance means slower decisions, procurement
is real above a certain size, and there is genuine reputational sensitivity
about overhead spend. Expect 2–4 months, not 2–4 weeks.

**Who to target:** UK charities with £5M+ annual income, approaching the Head
of Digital, Head of Fundraising Operations or Director of Operations — not the
CEO, and never a general enquiries inbox.
